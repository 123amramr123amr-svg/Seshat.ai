q: what is streaming generation | streaming generation
a: instead of composing the whole reply in one pass, tokens come out one at a time, each conditioned on everything before it. for long outputs the same sliding window applies: the generated tokens themselves flow into the fixed memory, so the reply can run to two million characters without hitting a hard ceiling. the user sees text arriving continuously rather than waiting for one giant block.

q: why do the first tokens matter so much | importance of early tokens
a: models concentrate a surprising share of attention on the earliest positions of context — they act as a distribution anchor. if those positions are evicted mid-stream, the softmax weights wander and quality degrades within a few thousand characters. keeping the first tokens permanently seated, which is exactly what attention sinks do, keeps the distribution stable for arbitrarily long streams.

q: what is an attention mask | attention mask
a: an attention mask is a grid of allowed and blocked pairs that keeps the model honest: a token may only look at positions at or before it, never ahead. streaming adds a second rule — only the window plus sinks are allowed for older positions. building the mask once per shape and caching it avoids recomputing a potentially huge grid on every step, which is a plain but real speed win during training.

q: what does chunked prefill mean | chunked prefill
a: prefill means reading the prompt before generating anything; chunked prefill does it in pieces instead of one enormous pass. each chunk's keys and values are written to memory seats, then the next chunk arrives. the seat layout never exceeds the window, so ram stays flat no matter the prompt size. once prefill ends, generation starts from the last position's distribution, having effectively attended across the entire prompt.

q: what is catastrophic forgetting | catastrophic forgetting
a: when a model trains heavily on new material, old skills can be overwritten — that is catastrophic forgetting. the standard defenses: mix old examples into the new training data, keep the learning rate small, take few steps, and re-measure the old benchmarks afterward with a quality gate. if the old accuracy drops beyond tolerance, restore the previous checkpoint and keep only the code changes. forgetting is normal; unmanaged forgetting is the bug.

q: walk me through exactly what happens when i send you a big document — from the moment i hit send until your reply appears. | document journey
a: it starts with slicing: the document is cut into small chunks and each chunk's characters become numbered tokens. then streaming: each chunk flows into the streaming memory in order, keys and values written into fixed seats — the first seats belong to the sink tokens that never leave, and behind them the window seats rotate, each new chunk overwriting the oldest one. through the whole stream i see the newest part in full detail plus the anchored first tokens, so the thread never breaks no matter how long the document runs. when your question arrives on the last line, generation begins token by token: each new token enters memory and prepares the distribution for the next, until the reply completes. the result: you can send a book-length document with a question at the end, and nothing overflows and no character silently vanishes.

q: if my document is a sales report full of tables and lists and lots of numbers, do you handle it correctly or do numbers get mixed up? | tables and numbers
a: they keep their arrangement exactly as sent — a table streams line by line and columns hold their positions, so the row-to-column relationship never slips. when you ask what the downtown branch sold in the third quarter, i take a small journey: find the line holding the branch name, take the cell at the crossing of the third-quarter column with that row, and answer with the number exactly as written — no guessing, no rounding. the same applies to numbers inside a long list. if the question is a comparison, i bring both numbers and compare them in front of you. my rule is simple: a number travels from your document to my reply character by character, and if the number you ask about is not in the document, i say it is not there instead of inventing a pretty-looking one.

Q: beginner at the gym | first time at the gym | workout plan for beginners
A: Start with three days a week: a push day (chest, shoulders, triceps), a pull day (back, biceps), and a legs-and-core day, three sets each — learning correct form before heavy weights. Form is the best investment in the first two months; the weight increases by itself after that.
